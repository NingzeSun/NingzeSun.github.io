    Abstract

    Bimanual manipulation requires two arms to divide roles over time, track task progress, and ground actions in a shared scene. Policies that map a joint observation to a joint action couple the two arms inside a single network, so neither agent can be perturbed or delayed on its own. We present Bi-SKAD, a bimanual diffusion policy under centralized training with decentralized execution. The two arms are separate diffusion policies acting on their own observations, coupled at execution through a communication channel. We introduce two modules that make this decomposition work: a semantic-kinematic affordance encoder that predicts where each arm and its partner should act, and a phase-based 3D trace that guides an arm's spatial motion. On selected RoboTwin 2.0 tasks and on real hardware, Bi-SKAD is comparable with fine-tuned monolithic vision-language-action baselines while remaining robust when one agent's sensing is corrupted or its motion is delayed. We further quantify how much the communication informs an arm's own action.

    Method

    We cast bimanual manipulation as a decentralized partially observable Markov decision process with one agent per arm. Both agents see the shared top camera and the instruction; each also sees its own hand camera and proprioception. At every step, agent \(i\) broadcasts a compact message holding its state, its gripper-closure bit, and its predicted trace, and samples its action conditioned on its own observation and the message it receives. The joint policy therefore factorizes into two per-arm policies that share no parameters:

    \[ \pi(\mathbf{a}_t \mid s_t) = \pi^1_{\theta^1}\!\left(a^1_t \mid o^1_t, m^2_t\right)\, \pi^2_{\theta^2}\!\left(a^2_t \mid o^2_t, m^1_t\right) \] \[ m^i_t = \left(\mathbf{q}^i_t,\; b^i_t,\; \hat{\tau}^i_t\right) \]
    Trained jointly (centralized training), executed separately (decentralized execution).
    Where to act

    Semantic-kinematic affordance encoder

    From frozen DINOv3 and SigLIP 2 features, the arm's state, and its partner's closure bit, a transformer predicts two heatmaps: where this arm should act and where its partner should act. A validity head predicts which arm the current affordance belongs to. Targets mark the next contact, masked to the contacted part with SAM 2.

    How to move

    Phase-based 3D trace

    Each arm predicts \(K\) points \((u, v, d)\), a top-camera pixel plus depth, from its gripper to the target of its current motion phase. The predictor reads SKAE's affordance-supervised tokens and decoded affordance, so it inherits SKAE's robustness to unseen scenes.

    How to act together

    Communication and DiT policy

    The partner's state and closure bit join the arm's conditioning state, and its trace is fused with the arm's own. A diffusion transformer then denoises that arm's action chunk. Real-time chunking keeps consecutive chunks consistent during closed-loop execution.

    Simulation Results

    We test on 15 bimanual tasks of RoboTwin 2.0. Each task trains on 550 demonstrations (50 clean and 500 randomized) and is evaluated on held-out OOD scenes with unseen object placements, lighting, and backgrounds: 30 rollouts per entry (15 clean and 15 randomized). We compare against five retrained architecture ablations, the decentralized diffusion policy LatentToM (LToM), and the fine-tuned monolithic vision-language-action models π0.5 and DM0.5.

    Task success

    With no fault injected, Bi-SKAD averages 79%, level with π0.5 (80%) and slightly behind DM0.5 (87%), while LToM reaches only 12% on these OOD scenes.

    Removing communication costs only 5 points, since the shared top camera and SKAE's partner prediction still carry implicit coordination. Centralized execution reaches 75%, so factorizing the policy does not degrade performance. Without partner supervision SKAE assigns roles less reliably (66%), and removing the affordance or the trace drops success to 53% and 39%, mostly on precise placement tasks.

    Per-task success rates (%)

    Fault isolation

    At serve time, one agent's proprioception receives Gaussian noise and its own hand camera is replaced by random pixels; the shared top camera and instruction are untouched. We report the intact arm's own subtask success.

    Bi-SKAD keeps the intact arm at 64%, well above the monoliths DM0.5 (30%) and π0.5 (18%), whose joint conditioning passes the fault to both arms. The no-communication variant reaches 82%; the gap is the cost of the channel, which passes the corrupted state to the intact agent.

    Per-task intact-arm subtask success (%)

    Asynchronous execution

    One arm's commanded action is held at its current pose for the first 40 environment steps. Both arms' states stay intact, so we report full-task success.

    Bi-SKAD is the best method at 75%, losing only 4 points from its no-fault success, against 26 points for π0.5 and 34 points for DM0.5, whose joint chunks assume the arms move in lockstep.

    Value of communication

    How much does the received message inform an agent's own action, given its local inputs \(o^i\)? Keeping those inputs fixed, we sample actions with messages drawn uniformly from four recorded candidates and estimate the Barber–Agakov lower bound

    \[ I\!\left(a^i; m^{-i} \mid o^i\right) \geq \mathbb{E}\!\left[\log \frac{q_{\phi}(m^{-i} \mid a^i, o^i)}{p(m^{-i} \mid o^i)}\right] \]

    Bi-SKAD averages 0.92 nats of a maximum \(\ln 4 \approx 1.39\), so each agent's action depends on the message it receives. Estimates saturate on grab roller and lift pot, where the arms hold one object together, and are lowest on hanging mug and dump bin bigbin, where they act mostly in turn. The no-communication variant receives no message, so its information is zero.
